1C–1 Finding the refractive index of an unknown material1C Refraction · 7 materials · 9 stepsOpen (Word)
AimThe aim of this practical activity is to calculate the refractive index of an unknown material by comparing the incident and reflected rays.
Materials
  • Hodson light box
  • Battery pack or power supply
  • Lenses
  • Single slit plate
  • Paper protractors (2 semi-circle images or one full circular image)
  • Pencil
  • Graph paper
Method
  1. Set up the Hodson light box with a single slit adjacent to the light source.
  2. Place a paper protractor with the rounded edge facing the light in front of the light box.
  3. Place a second protractor so it makes a full circle. Alternatively, print the image in the appendix to use as a protractor.
  4. Initially aim the light along the normal (at 90 degrees) so that with no change the light will continue along the line of the normal.
  5. Place the transparent object that you are measuring at the diameter of the protractor circle.
  6. Modify the angle of incidence to all angles between 10 and 80 degrees. Keep in mind 10 degrees means 10 away from 90 and so on. You must calculate from the normal.
  7. Record the angle of refraction for each from your paper protractor.
  8. Graph the sin  (i)  against the sin  (r)  for your calculations. Find the gradient of this graph. This will give you the refractive index of the unknown material.
  9. Research what the material might be made of based on the refractive index you calculate.

2A–1 Convection in water2A Thermal energy · 9 materials · 16 stepsOpen (Word)
AimTo make the convective flow in water visible and to investigate the conditions under which convection occurs in a liquid.
Materials
  • Bunsen burner for Part 1
  • 200 mL beaker
  • A straw or thin rigid tube
  • Tweezers
  • Crystals of potassium permanganate (KMnO₄)
  • Retort stand and heatproof mat
  • Heat lamp for Part 2 (or a desk lamp with incandescent bulb of at least 100 W)
  • Ice cubes for Part 3
  • Plastic bag and string or twist-ties for Part 3
SafetyCare should be taken when using Bunsen burners and heat lamps. Ensure that water is not spilled on the heat lamp. Be mindful that the beakers and water will be hot, so should be handled carefully and placed securely on the heatproof mat and retort stand. Allow it to cool before disposing of the permanganate solution.
Method
  1. Place the beaker of water on the retort stand.
  2. Put the straw in the water (at an oblique angle).
  3. Allow to stand undisturbed for 5 minutes.
  4. Use tweezers to drop only 2 or 3 potassium permanganate crystals down the straw so they settle at the bottom of the beaker, to the side.
  5. Carefully withdraw the straw, avoiding disturbing the water.
  6. Place the Bunsen burner to the beaker under the crystals, and light it.
  7. Observe and sketch the direction of currents in the water, inferred from movement of the purple colour.
  8. Place a beaker of fresh water on the bench.
  9. Place the heat lamp (turned off) so that it will shine on the top of the water surface.
  10. Use same procedure as in Part 1 Steps 2–5 with crystals and straw.
  11. Turn on the lamp.
  12. Observe and sketch the direction of inferred currents in the water as before.
  13. Place a beaker of fresh lukewarm water on the bench, with a retort stand over it.
  14. Follow Steps 2–5 of Part 1.
  15. Place some ice cubes in a plastic bag, squeeze out the air, and tie it closed with the string or twist-tie. Gently immerse the plastic bag in the water above the potassium permanganate crystals, so it hangs just below the water surface, and tie it to the retort stand.
  16. Observe and sketch the direction of inferred currents in the water as before.
Prelab questions
  1. What explains the change of density with temperature?
  2. Should liquid of lower density move up through liquid of higher density when the liquid is heated from below?
  3. Should liquid be heated from above or below if you want to minimise the amount of energy used to heat it?
  4. Explain whether a heating method that warms all parts of a liquid by the same amount at the same time, would result in convection currents.
  5. Examining the patterns of convection and the efficiency of heating from below in contrast to from above
  6. Compare the direction of the currents in Parts 1–3 of the experiment in the spaces below by drawing a diagram, including arrows for any currents observed and their direction. Below the diagram, summarise in words whether the water was heated or cooled at the top or the bottom, and describe any currents observed. (2 marks for each part)

Materials
  • 100 chocolate M&Ms (do not use peanut M&Ms), there are about 200 in a 180 g packet, so one packet should be enough for two groups. The trial 1 M&Ms could be re-used in trial 2, then only 80 will be needed for each group. (Other brands of lolly could be used, as long as they have an equal chance of landing on either side, i.e. they are symmetrical, and a mark that distinguishes the sides).
  • A cup
  • Clean sheets of paper or trays
  • Coloured pencils or textas for different plotlines on graphs
Method
  1. For trial 1, place 20 M&Ms into the cup.
  2. Shake the cup and pour the M&M’s onto the paper or tray.
  3. The M&Ms with the printed ‘M’ side up are regarded as ‘decayed’. Remove them from the trial and returning them to the packet. Record the number of ‘undecayed’ M&Ms remaining and in table in the Results section against ‘shake and pour’ event number 1. Record the next ‘shake and pour’ event as number 2, and so on.
  4. Return the undecayed M&Ms to the cup and repeat Steps 2 and 3, recording the number of undecayed M&Ms in the table in the Results section against the shake and pour event number, until there are none left, or until 12 events have been carried out.
  5. Repeat Steps 1–4 as the second trial, this time with 80 M&Ms.
  6. Plot a graph of the results from the table in the Results section on the grid provided, using different colours for the plotlines of the two trials. Include a key. Note that the axis for trial 1 is on the left, the axis for trial 2 is on the right and they are not to the same scale. This is so the shape of the curves can be compared at the same size.
  7. Use the graph to determine the half-life of M&Ms in terms of the number of pours.

AimTo investigate the shielding required for protection from external α-, β- and γ-radiation. <Teacher note: This practical requires the use of ionising radiation sources. Materials
  • Alpha, beta and gamma sources produced for school laboratory use, sealed and complete with safe handling instructions
  • Stand and clamp to hold source
  • Radiation detector with stand and clamp
  • Counter and powerpack for the detector
  • Sheets of cardboard, aluminium, lead and, at the teacher’s discretion, sheets of other materials such as cloth, polythene, rubber and a lead block
  • Stand to hold the sheets between source and detector. Some sheets may need to be held at the top rather than the bottom.
  • Tongs
  • Gloves
  • Stopwatch
  • Ruler
Safety- Care should be taken when using radioactive sources. Since radiation is invisible, it is important to be aware of the dangers involved. - Always handle radioactive sources with a pair of tongs and wear gloves. - Avoid having the sources close to your body. - Never damage the container in which the radioisotope is held. - Make sure all radioactive sources are in a lead lined container when not in use.
Method
  1. Set up the apparatus as shown in the diagram below.
  2. With all the radiation sources in their lead box, obtain a background radiation count by leaving the counter on for 3 minutes. Average this to calculate the number of counts per minute for the background value.
  3. Place the alpha source (open side toward detector tube) about 5–10 mm away from the end of the detector tube and record the number of counts in 1 minute, as timed by the stopwatch. Subtract the background value from the recorded value to get the actual count from the source.
  4. Now place a sheet of cardboard between the source and the tube. Record the counts in 1 minute and subtract the background count to get the actual count. Repeat this process with the other sheets chosen for the practical. Write names in the blank column headers in the table in the Results section.
  5. Replace the alpha source with a beta source (radiation symbol toward detector) and repeat Steps 2 and 3.
  6. Now replace the beta source with a gamma source (radiation symbol toward detector) and repeat Steps 2 and 3.
  7. Also, using the lead block if required, determine how many millimetres of lead are required to effectively stop the gamma rays completely (i.e. reduce the count to the background level).

9C–1 Spring constants and elastic potential energy9C Springs · 4 materials · 8 stepsOpen (Word)
AimTo find the spring constant and stored elastic potential energy for a 5 cm extension of three different springs.
Materials
  • Three springs each with a hook at one end and a ring at the other so they can be hung from an arm and a weight can be attached to them. Each should have a different spring constant, judged by feeling how much force is required to gently stretch them in your hands. Number the springs 1, 2 and 3.
  • Retort stand and clamp, plus a clamp to fix the base of the stand to the bench or table
  • Metre ruler
  • 50 g masses
Method
  1. Set up the first spring so that it is hanging vertically from an arm clamped to a retort stand and clamp as shown in the diagram below.
  2. Attach a ruler with sticky tape or other methods so it hangs vertically next to the spring, with the 0 graduation aligned with the top of the first coil of the spring. Then, the changing length of the spring can be read off the ruler.
  3. Measure the unextended length of the spring with no masses attached by noting where the bottom of the last coil aligns with the ruler. Record in Table 1 in the Results section.
  4. Hang a 50 g mass from the end of the spring and record how much it stretches (∆x) in the results table provided below.
  5. Add another 50 g mass to the spring and record the new value of ∆x. Note that this is the change in length from the unextended length.
  6. Repeat Step 5 two more times, so that an extra mass of 200 g total has been added to the spring.
  7. Repeat Steps 3–6 for each spring and record the results in Table 2 and 3 in the Results section.
  8. Complete the last column of each table in the Results section by calculating the force, F newtons, applied to the spring by the mass, m kilograms, using F = mg with g = 9.8 m s^(–2).
